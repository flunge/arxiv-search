% “The authors claim that their method can be used as a drop-in replacement for any tracing-based method. The exact meaning of this statement is unclear and would benefit from further explanation.” - \textbf{Csak}
The main contribution of our paper is a novel \emph{gradient computation} algorithm that enables fully sorting-free, stochastic ray tracing for 3DGS \emph{optimization}, particularly, making \emph{the backward pass} efficient and practical. The forward rendering pass (Alg. 1), on the other hand, follows Sun et al., and the timing breakdown in Table 2 aims to identify the performance gain brought by our improvement to the backward pass. 
To avoid misunderstanding, we will further clarify in the paper our core contribution.

We clarify that our method intentionally introduces only a single substantive design choice: the two-sample stochastic gradient estimator. The corresponding single-sample formulation is identical to the one proposed in StochasticSplats, which is already included as a baseline in our experiments (supplemental Table~1). All other components are held identical, ensuring the comparison isolates the effect of the gradient estimator alone. We will explicitly frame this comparison as an ablation study, and move it to the main text.

For the NVS task, the training setup is identical to 3DGRT, except when explicitly specified in Sec.~5 of the paper. As described there, we set the densification interval to 400 to ensure that our algorithm generates a similar number of Gaussian particles. For \textsl{MipNeRF360} dataset in Table~1, the final average Gaussian count is 2.12M for our method, and 1.95M for 3DGRT. We will include a more detailed per-scene metric reporting for the NVS tasks in a revised version.

For comparison to StochasticSplats, we use 30spp for both methods, following the setting from the NVS comparisons in the main paper.

\myparagraph{Gaussian indexing (\Rone).}
% The writing in Sec 3 (L175-L191) creates some confusion in terms notation for ordering. L177 says the ordering i=1,2,3 is arbitrary, but much of the subsequent text treats this as a sorted ordering and writes i < j etc. (need to explain meaning of $\prec$)
We use an override symbol $i\prec j$ (as opposed to $<$) to denote the $i^{\text{th}}$ Gaussian being in the front of the $j^{\text{th}}$ Gaussian. Such ordering of depth is different from the indexing of Gaussians (which we assume to be arbitrary).
